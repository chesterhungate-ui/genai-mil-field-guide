You don’t have to love AI to get value from it. Treat it like a sharp new action officer: useful from day one, but you still check the work.

01 / My take

Seven ideas

My opinion, one idea at a time. Each one is a sentence to remember, then the why.

New things are a risk.Changing how you work takes effort, and you don’t know how it will turn out. Expect to try, adjust and try again.
This is a starting point.There are more AI tips out there than anyone can use. This page narrows it to the tools we can actually open.
  1. 1

    The AI proposes. You approve.

    I believe that within 30 years, AI will handle most of our day-to-day thinking. What stays human is judgment and skill. Build the habit now: a person checks anything that is expensive, public, permanent or about a person.

  2. 2

    Grok’s skills are our most underused feature.

    Grok can produce a finished Word document, PDF, deck or spreadsheet, and run a task every morning before you arrive. Most people never find it, because it’s behind the + button in the chat box.

  3. 3

    Hand it the whole job, not just a question.

    A chat is a consultant: you get an opinion, then you still do the work. A project or agent is an analyst at your desk: it opens your files and builds the draft. You’re ready when you keep re-pasting the same background.

  4. 4

    One person can build a tool the whole office uses.

    Do the job once by hand. When it works, save it as a custom GPT, a Gemini agent or a Grok skill. Schedule it only after it runs cleanly twice. Anyone with the share link gets what you loaded into it, so check the files first.

  5. 5

    Give it the GIST.

    Four things every prompt needs. Give the AI the gist, and it can do the job.

    1. GGoalWhat you want done, in one sentence.
    2. IInfoThe background: who it’s for, and why.
    3. SShapeThe product: one page, a table, five slides.
    4. TTrapsWhat to avoid: jargon, guessing, length.
    5. ✓“GIST check”Grok flags what’s missing first. Get the skill

    A good prompt gets you about halfway on the first try. Read the answer, fix the prompt, run it again.

  6. 6

    Show it, don’t describe it.

    Upload the actual spreadsheet, slide or memo instead of retyping it. Every time you put a file into your own words, detail gets lost.

  7. 7

    Know when it’s guessing.

    With web search off, the AI only knows what it learned up to its training date. Turn search on for anything current, and check every citation.

02 / My rules

Eight rules

Short habits that get a better answer the first time.

  1. 1
    One goal per prompt

    Give the goal and the context, then tell it to ask you questions before it starts.

  2. 2
    Say what “done” looks like

    Length, format and audience. Otherwise it guesses.

  3. 3
    Fix the prompt, not the answer

    Arguing in the chat piles on confusion. Edit your prompt and run it again.

  4. 4
    Don’t lead the witness

    Ask neutral questions. Ask “this is right, isn’t it?” and it will usually agree.

  5. 5
    Ask for twenty ideas, ranked

    Have it score how likely a typical AI is to suggest each one. The new thinking is in the low scores. Use this prompt.

  6. 6
    Ask for the real file

    Need a deck or a spreadsheet? Have Grok build it, not describe it.

  7. 7
    One project per line of effort

    Keep memory short and accurate, and never let it store PII.

  8. 8
    Start fresh when it drifts

    When a long chat gets worse, ask for a summary and paste it into a new chat.

04 / Prompts

Prompts for our work

Written for a marketing office. Every detail is notional, so swap in your own unclassified facts, never names or PII.

Turn a meeting into taskersCopilot tab in Teams, after a recorded meeting. Or paste your notes.Live demo
Recap the [meeting name] meeting for my director. Start with a three-sentence bottom line. Then give me: decisions made, a table of taskers (task, owner by role, due date) and open questions. Flag any tasker with no owner or no date. Keep it under 200 words.
Tighten a note to an agency teamPaste your draft into Copilot in Teams or Outlook.
Rewrite my draft below so it is shorter, clearer, and polite but firm. Put the ask in the first sentence. Keep it under 100 words. Give me two versions: a formal email and a short Teams message.

Draft: Hi team, thanks for round two of the creative, we have some feedback, overall it's moving in the right direction but leadership felt the headline on concept B is a little confusing and the call to action isn't clear, also we need the 15-second cut by the 20th instead of the 27th because of the event, can you let us know if that works or if we need to talk timeline.
Catch up on a long email threadIn Outlook, open the thread, then ask Copilot.
Summarize this email thread in five bullets: what's been decided, what's still open, and what's being asked of me, and by when. Then draft a two-sentence reply that answers the ask.
Audience research with sourcesA public topic, so the search can help.Live demo
Find the most recent public research (from the last two years) on how 17- to 24-year-olds in the U.S. discover and trust information about careers: which platforms, which messengers, and what turns them off. Summarize it in six bullets I can put in a creative brief. Give the source and date for each bullet, and tell me which points you could not confirm.
Compare monthly reports: what changed?Choose Deep Research, attach several unclassified reports, then run this.
Read the attached monthly performance reports. In a table, show how leads, cost per lead and conversion rate changed month to month, and which channel drove each change. Cite the report and page for every number. End with three questions I should ask the analytics team before the next review.
Build an agent: Creative Brief BuilderPaste into Agent Designer’s description box. Test it in Preview before you share it.
Create an agent called "Creative Brief Builder." When I give it rough, unclassified notes about a marketing need, it writes a one-page creative brief with these headings: Objective, Audience, Key insight, Single-minded message, Proof points, Deliverables, Mandatories, Timeline, and How we'll measure it. It uses only what I provide, marks gaps as TBD, asks up to three questions when the objective or audience is unclear, and never includes names or personal information.
Event notes to an after-action reviewUpload your notes. A photo of handwritten notes works too.
Turn the attached event notes into an after-action review I can download as a Word document. Use these headings: event and purpose, what happened, sustain (three things), improve (three things), metrics (leads, conversations, cost) and recommendations for next time, each with an owner by role. Use only what's in my notes, mark missing details as TBD, and leave out names and personal information.
Build a full campaign-recap deckGISTA finished 7-slide PowerPoint in a bold black-and-gold style.Live demo
  1. Step 1Open Grok on genai.mil and start a new chat.
  2. Step 2Click + in the chat box and add the PowerPoint skill.
  3. Step 3Paste the prompt. Answer its questions.
  4. Step 4Download the file. Then refine it: “Make slide 4 a bar chart.”
GOAL: Build a finished 7-slide PowerPoint deck I can brief from: a notional end-of-quarter recap of a national awareness campaign aimed at 17- to 24-year-olds.

INFO: The audience is senior leaders in a marketing office. It's a 10-minute brief that ends in a decision. Use only these notional numbers: impressions 48 million (goal 40 million); site visits 1.2 million; leads 18,400 (goal 20,000); cost per lead $62 (goal $55); best channel: short-form video; weakest channel: display ads.

SHAPE:
Slide 1: title and a one-sentence bottom line.
Slide 2: bottom line up front: three results and the one decision needed.
Slide 3: results against goals, as a table marked green or red.
Slide 4: what worked: the top three channels, ranked, with one chart.
Slide 5: what didn't work and why, in three bullets.
Slide 6: next quarter: three recommendations, each with its cost and risk.
Slide 7: decisions needed.
Add speaker notes of under 60 words to every slide.
Style: bold and high-contrast. Black (#121011) backgrounds, gold (#FFCC01) headlines and accents, tan (#D6CEB5) secondary text, white body text. Heavy condensed all-caps headlines, one big number per slide where it fits, plenty of empty space, simple line icons. Put a gray box labeled PHOTO wherever a photo would go.

TRAPS: No Army logos, seals, emblems or slogans. No real names, PII or non-public data. Don't invent numbers: if something is missing, write [TBD] and list it at the end. No more than four bullets a slide, no bullet longer than 12 words, and no jargon.

Before you build, ask me up to three questions if anything is unclear. Then build the .pptx file for me to download.
Build a GIST Check skillTurn on Skill creator and paste this. Then end any prompt with “GIST check.”

In ChatGPT Mil, you can paste this into Custom instructions instead.

Help me build a skill called "GIST Check." Use it whenever my prompt includes the words "GIST check." Before doing any work, check my prompt for four parts: Goal (what exactly I want done), Info (the background, including who it's for), Shape (the format and length, such as one page, a table or five slides) and Traps (what to avoid). Reply first with a short table that lists each part as Clear or Missing, with one line on what's missing, such as "no audience" or "no length." Then ask me up to three questions to fill the gaps, and show a better version of my prompt with the gaps marked [like this]. Don't start the task until I answer or say "go." If all four parts are clear, say "GIST complete" and do the task. Ask me any questions you need before you build it.
A weekly trade-press read-aheadOpen a project, choose Automations, and use this as the task.
Every Monday at 0700, search the web for public news and trade-press stories from the last seven days about recruitment marketing, advertising to Gen Z, and major changes on social media platforms. Write a one-page read-ahead: a two-sentence bottom line, then up to six bullets, each with its source and date, then one line on what this could mean for a marketing office like ours. Use public sources only.
Analyze a campaign spreadsheetSwitch to Expert mode and upload an unclassified Excel or CSV file. It may take a few minutes.
Analyze the attached campaign spreadsheet. Tell me in three sentences what it shows. Then list the three biggest trends or outliers with the numbers behind them, build one chart of the most important trend, and give me a cleaned-up Excel file to download. List any data problems you found, such as blanks or duplicates.
Design an A/B testExpert mode. Good for anyone who isn’t an analyst.
Help me design an A/B test for a paid social ad. The notional goal is more sign-ups for information about part-time service. Give me: the hypothesis, two variants (what changes and what stays the same), the one metric that decides the winner, how long to run it and roughly how many people each variant needs, and what result would mean "no real difference." Explain it so someone who isn't an analyst can follow.
Teach it your format onceSkill creator. Attach one good, unclassified example first.
Help me build a skill called "Our Brief Format." Use the attached example as the template: copy its headings, order, length and tone exactly. When I give it rough notes, it fills in that format, keeps every fact from my notes, marks anything missing as TBD, and adds nothing I didn't provide. Ask me any questions you need before you build it.
See why web search mattersAsk this twice: once with the Web switch off, then on. Compare.
What is today's date, who is the current President of the United States, and what is the most recent date your information covers?
Social posts in three lengthsStart a new chat. Set reasoning to Medium.Live demo
Write social posts announcing a notional career-day booth at a college football game this Saturday, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., at Gate C. The audience is students aged 18 to 24 and their parents. Give me three versions: a 40-word post, a 120-word post and a three-line caption for a short video. Each needs a clear call to action and suggested alt text for the image. Keep it plain and upbeat, with no slang that will age badly. Flag any claim I should verify before posting.
Custom GPT: a brand-voice checkerPaste into the custom GPT’s instructions. Add your unclassified style guide as a file.

Keep it on “Only me” until you’ve removed anything not everyone should see.

You check draft copy for a marketing office. Compare each draft against the attached, unclassified brand and writing guidelines, and say which guideline each comment comes from. Reply with a one-line verdict, then up to five fixes (tone, reading level, clarity of the call to action, inclusive language), then a revised version. If the guidelines don't cover something, say so and don't guess. Never store or repeat names or personal information.
Talking points and tough questionsFor a public panel, career fair or industry day.
I'm speaking for five minutes on a public industry panel about reaching young audiences. Write five talking points in plain English, each under 25 words. Then list the eight toughest questions the audience might ask, with a short, honest answer to each. Mark anything I should confirm with public affairs before I say it.
Set your custom instructions onceSettings, then Personalization, then Custom instructions.
I work in a marketing office. Write in plain English for busy leaders. Always start with a one- or two-sentence bottom line. Use short numbered points instead of long paragraphs. When I ask for copy, give me two options. If you're not sure about something, tell me instead of guessing, and list what I should check.
Shape an idea into a one-page conceptSet reasoning to High first.
Help me turn a rough idea into a one-page concept for my boss. The idea: a shared, unclassified calendar so every regional team can see upcoming events, campaign launches and asset deadlines in one place. Use these headings: Problem, Proposed solution, Who uses it, What success looks like in 90 days, Risks, and Decisions needed. Ask me up to three questions first if something important is missing.
Twenty ideas, ranked by how obvious they areWorks in any tool. Replace the brackets with your problem.
Give me 20 ideas for [your problem, for example: reaching young adults who have never considered public service]. For each idea, estimate the percent chance that a typical AI assistant would suggest it, from 0 to 100 percent. Sort the list from most likely to least likely. At least eight ideas should be under 10 percent. Then pick the two low-percentage ideas you think are most worth trying, and tell me in one sentence each why.
Same prompt, side by sideRun this in each tool and compare the answers.
In 120 words or less, explain to a new staff officer what CUI is, what it is not, and one common mistake people make when handling it. End with one question I should ask my security manager.

Words you’ll hear

Prompt
What you type to the AI. Say who it's for, what you want and the format.
AI model
The engine behind the chat box. Gemini, GPT and Grok are models from three different companies.
Agent
An assistant that carries out several steps on its own, such as reading notes and drafting a summary.
Custom GPT
Your own version of ChatGPT, set up once with instructions and files for one job. Only ChatGPT Mil has these.
Skill
A saved recipe for one kind of job, such as “make a PowerPoint,” that the AI follows the same way every time.
Automation
A task the AI runs by itself on a schedule, such as every weekday at 0700.
Deep research
The AI spends several minutes searching and reading, then writes a long report with citations.
Hallucination
When the AI states something false with confidence. That's why a person checks every output.
Training cutoff
The date the AI stopped learning. Turn on web search for anything newer.
CUI and IL5
CUI is sensitive but unclassified information. IL5 is the security level genai.mil is approved for: it covers CUI, not classified.
All 26 terms
GPT
Short for Generative Pre-trained Transformer, OpenAI's name for the model behind ChatGPT.
Everyone can chat
A ChatGPT Mil setting that makes a share link anyone on GenAI.mil can use. Remove sensitive files first.
Reasoning effort
How hard the AI thinks before it answers. Higher is slower but more careful.
Manager agent
A Gemini agent that splits a job and hands the pieces to other agents, like a project manager.
Knowledge file
A file you attach so the AI answers from your material, such as an SOP or a spreadsheet.
RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)
The AI looks up the right passages in your files before it answers, like an open-book test.
Multimodal
The AI can take in pictures, scans, audio and video, not just text.
Project or workspace
A folder that keeps related chats, files and instructions together.
Team space
A shared area in Grok where colleagues use the same projects and skills.
Custom instructions
Standing rules the AI follows in every chat, such as “lead with the bottom line.”
Memory
The AI remembers facts about you between chats. Check what it saved, and never let it store PII.
Connector
A plug that lets the AI read from a system you already use, such as email or a shared drive.
Token and context window
A token is a small piece of a word. The context window is how much the AI can read at once.
Sandbox
A walled-off computer the AI uses to run code and build files without touching anything else.
Web search index
The copy of the internet a tool searches. It may be a few hours behind the live web.
Citation
A link to where the answer came from. Click it and check it.
