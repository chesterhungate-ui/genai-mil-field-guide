The one-stop guide to AI at work.

What each tool on your government computer does, the rules I follow, and prompts you can try today: Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT Mil, Gemini for Government and Grok for Government.

By MAJ Hungate, Army Enterprise Marketing Office. The views here are my own and are not those of the Army or DoD.

Before you paste anything: GenAI.mil is approved for unclassified work, up to and including CUI. Never put in classified information, PII or health information. Copilot follows your DoD 365 rules. And a person checks every output before it leaves the office.

My take

Most of us open these tools, type a question and stop there. We're leaving the most useful parts unused.

  1. Grok's skills and automations are the most underused feature we have. Grok can produce a finished Word document, PDF, slide deck or spreadsheet, and it can run a task every morning before you arrive. Most people never click the + button to find them.
  2. One person can build a tool the whole office uses. A custom GPT or a Gemini agent turns your section's know-how into something anyone can ask. In Gemini, a manager agent can even split the work among specialist agents: one researches, one analyzes, one writes the summary. That's also the risk: anyone on GenAI.mil who gets the share link gets what you loaded into it, so check the files first. You can turn a link off later.
  3. Match the tool to the job. Copilot is for quick edits inside Teams and Outlook. Go to genai.mil when you need to think, research or build.
  4. Know when it's guessing. With web search off, the AI only knows what it learned up to its training date. Turn search on for anything current, and check every citation.

My rules

I adapted these from Ruben Hassid's "Claude 101" (How to AI) and rewrote them for the tools on genai.mil.

  1. One goal per prompt. Give it the goal and the context, then tell it to ask you questions before it starts.
  2. Say what "done" looks like. Length, format and audience. Otherwise it guesses.
  3. Fix the prompt, not the answer. If an answer misses, edit your prompt and run it again. Don't argue with it in the chat.
  4. Don't lead the witness. Ask neutral questions. If you ask "this is right, isn't it?", it will usually agree.
  5. Ask for twenty ideas, and make it rank them. The first five will be the obvious ones every AI gives. Have it put a percentage on each idea: how likely a typical AI would be to suggest it. Then look hard at the low-percentage ones. That’s where the new thinking is. Use this prompt.
  6. Ask for the real file. If you need a deck or a spreadsheet, have Grok build it, not describe it.
  7. One project per line of effort. Keep memory short and accurate, and never let it store PII.
  8. Start fresh when it drifts. When a long chat gets worse, ask for a summary and paste it into a new chat.

Where to find them

genai.mil

Open genai.mil on your government computer and sign in with your CAC. This one page is where you pick Gemini, ChatGPT Mil or Grok.

The menu across the top has About, Training, Events and Resources. Start with Training.

You'll also see an API key setting. That is for approved developers only, and you don't need it to use the tools.

Copilot

Copilot is not on genai.mil. In Teams, click the Copilot tab on the far left. It is also in Outlook.

It opens as a simple chat. You can start new chats, go back to recent ones, and change settings.

Pick a tool in 10 seconds

Copilot

Best for: quick edits, and summarizing your own meetings, chats and email.

  • Summarizes Teams meetings, chats, emails and documents, and pulls out action items
  • Finds files, people and chats you already have access to, and never past your permissions
  • No internet and no images in the government version
  • Help inside Word, PowerPoint and Excel only if your office has that license

Where: the Copilot tab on the left side of Teams, or in Outlook. Not on GenAI.mil.

ChatGPT Mil

Best for: writing documents and building your own helper.

  • Custom GPTs: your own assistant, loaded with your files
  • Browse GPTs others built in Explore GPTs
  • Projects keep files and chats together, and take whole folders
  • Choose how hard it thinks: Instant, Low, Medium or High
  • Helper GPTs pick the right feature and coach your prompts
  • Your prompts and files aren't used to train the model

Where: GenAI.mil, since 31 Aug 2026

Gemini

Best for: long research, big stacks of documents, and building agents.

  • Reads documents, spreadsheets, images, audio and video, even scanned handwritten notes
  • Holds about 1 million tokens at once, so it can take a very large pile of files
  • Deep Research writes long reports with citations you can check
  • Agent Designer: build an agent in plain English, run it on a schedule, and let a manager agent hand work to other agents
  • Ready-made agents for AARs, information papers, meeting summaries and staff estimates

Where: GenAI.mil, since 9 Dec 2025

Grok

Best for: making real files and running tasks on a schedule.

  • Skills make Word, PDF, PowerPoint and Excel files
  • Automations run a task for you every day at a set time
  • Team spaces share projects and skills with colleagues
  • Auto, Fast (Grok 4.3) or Expert (Grok 4.5). Grok 4.6 is in beta
  • Shows its sources, and Expert mode runs code to build files and crunch data
  • Expect waits on Expert jobs and limits on file size for now

Where: GenAI.mil, since 31 Aug 2026

Free training on genai.mil

Open the Training and Events tabs on genai.mil. You'll find live sessions and on-demand courses for all three tools: Gemini, ChatGPT Mil and Grok.

  • ChatGPT Mil has the most courses. Its training page links to the ChatGPT Mil help center (help.openai.mil, on the government network only).
  • There are 8 long videos. Topics include getting started, chats, citations, files, personalization and memory, a quick start workshop for custom GPTs, and the ready-made Acquisition GPT.
  • It also has prompt packs for different roles, short workshops, quick reference sheets, how-to guides, and the recordings, slides and prompts from past sessions.
  • Good first course: the custom GPT quick start workshop.
  • Grok has its own help center (docs.grok.genai.mil, on the government network only). It covers getting started, chat, creating documents, skills, automations, projects, attachments, settings and policies, and it has a user guide PDF.
  • Gemini has self-paced courses, live workshops, office hours and training on building agents. Start with an agent-building session.
  • In Grok, the Help menu also has a Feedback button that goes to the genai.mil team. Use it when something breaks or is missing.

Words you'll hear, in plain English

AI modelThe engine behind the chat box. Gemini, GPT and Grok are models from three different companies. Each one writes answers by predicting the most likely next words.
PromptWhat you type to the AI. Clear prompts get better answers: say who it's for, what you want, and the format.
GPTShort for Generative Pre-trained Transformer. Generative means it creates new text. Pre-trained means it already learned from a huge amount of writing before you ever used it. Transformer is the type of design inside it. GPT is OpenAI's name, so it belongs to ChatGPT.
Custom GPTYour own version of ChatGPT, set up for one job. You give it instructions and reference files once, and anyone you share it with can just ask it questions. On genai.mil, only ChatGPT Mil has these.
Everyone can chatA ChatGPT Mil sharing setting. It creates a link to your custom GPT that you send to people yourself. Anyone with GenAI.mil access who has the link can chat with it, and you can turn the link off. Before you share, remove any file that not everyone with the link should see.
Reasoning effortHow hard the AI thinks before it answers. In ChatGPT Mil: Instant, Low, Medium or High. In Grok: Fast or Expert, or Auto to let it choose. Higher is slower but more careful, so save it for analysis rather than quick rewrites.
AgentAn assistant that can carry out several steps on its own, such as reading notes, sorting tasks and drafting a summary. Gemini's Agent Designer lets you build one in plain English.
Manager agentIn Gemini's Agent Designer, an agent that splits a job and hands the pieces to other agents (sub-agents), like a project manager assigning tasks to specialists. For example, one researches, one analyzes and one summarizes.
Knowledge fileA file you attach to a chat or agent so it answers from your material, such as an SOP, doctrine or a spreadsheet. In Gemini agents the whole file is placed in front of the AI, so keep knowledge files short and on topic.
Deep researchA mode where the AI spends several minutes searching and reading, then writes a long report with citations. Use it for a full picture, not a quick answer.
MultimodalThe AI can take in more than text: pictures, scans, audio and video. Gemini can read a photo of handwritten notes and turn it into a summary.
Project or workspaceA folder that keeps related chats, files and instructions together, so you don't start from zero each time.
Team spaceA shared area in Grok that colleagues invite you to. Everyone in it can use the same projects and skills. Switch between your personal space and your teams by clicking your name.
AutomationA task the AI runs by itself on a schedule, such as every weekday at 0700. In Grok you set one up inside a project. For example, a daily roundup of public news on a topic you track.
Custom instructionsStanding rules the AI follows in every chat, such as "always lead with the bottom line."
MemoryThe AI remembers facts about you and your work between chats. Useful, but check what it has saved, and never let it store PII.
ConnectorA plug that lets the AI reach outside the chat into a system you already use, such as email, a calendar or a shared drive, and read from it for you. Without one, the AI only knows what you type or upload. Copilot's cloud files work this way. In Grok, only admins can add connectors for now.
SkillA saved recipe for one kind of job, such as "make a PowerPoint" or "write an executive summary." Turn it on in a chat and the AI follows that recipe every time. Grok comes with skills for Word, PDF, PowerPoint, Excel and video files, and you can build your own just by describing it to Skill creator. A skill can include example files, such as a sample memo, so every output follows the same format. Build several small skills rather than one giant one. Teams can share their skills with each other.
Token and context windowA token is a small piece of a word. The context window is how many tokens the AI can keep in mind at once. Gemini's is about 1 million tokens, enough for a few thousand pages, so you can give it a big stack of files in one go.
SandboxA walled-off practice computer the AI can use to run code, build files or crunch numbers, without touching anything else. Grok's Expert mode uses one, which is how it makes finished spreadsheets and slide decks.
Training cutoffThe date the AI stopped learning. Without web search it doesn't know anything newer, so it may give an old answer about current events. Turn web search on for anything recent.
Web search indexThe copy of the internet a tool searches. ChatGPT Mil and Gemini both search a copy kept inside the secure government environment instead of the live web, so very recent news may be a few hours behind.
CitationA link to where the answer came from. Click it, and check that it says what the AI claims.
HallucinationWhen the AI states something false with confidence. This is why a person checks every output.
CUI and IL5CUI is Controlled Unclassified Information: sensitive but not classified. IL5 is the security level genai.mil is approved for, which covers CUI. It does not cover classified information.

Seed prompts: try each tool on its strength

Every example is notional. Swap in your own unclassified details, never names or PII.

Copilot: tighten a Teams message or emailPaste your draft into the Copilot tab in Teams.
Rewrite my draft below so it is shorter and clearer for a senior leader. Put the ask in the first sentence. Keep it professional and under 80 words. Give me two versions: one formal, one casual for a Teams chat.

Draft: Hey sir, wanted to touch base on the training schedule thing from last week, we still need a decision on whether land nav is a half day or a full day because S4 needs to lock in buses by Monday and I think a half day is probably fine but wanted your thoughts.
Copilot: pull taskers from a file in your cloudAdd the file as a source first: a cloud file from Teams or OneDrive.
From the attached file, list every tasker with the owner's role and the due date, in a table. Then give me a two-sentence bottom line for my boss. Flag anything with no owner or no date.
ChatGPT Mil: turn rough notes into a memoStart a Project, paste your notes, run this.
You are a staff writer. Turn the notes below into a one-page information memo. Use a bottom line up front, then three to five numbered points, then a recommendation. Plain English, active voice, sentences under 25 words. If something is missing, list the questions I need to answer instead of guessing.

Notes: Range day moved from Tuesday to Thursday because of weather. S4 to confirm bus support by Monday. Company A needs 40 more sets of eye protection. Decision needed: land navigation refresher half day or full day.
ChatGPT Mil: build a custom GPT for your work streamPaste into the custom GPT's instructions box, then add your unclassified reference files.

Tip: add two or three starter prompts for questions your team asks every week, and turn on web search if the answers need current information. Keep it on "Only me" until you've removed anything not everyone should see.

You are the go-to helper for a notional battalion S3 training section. Answer questions using the attached reference files first, and say which file each answer came from. If the files don't cover the question, say so plainly and don't guess. Keep answers short: a bottom line up front, then up to five bullets. When someone asks for a product, such as a training schedule or a memo, ask up to three questions before you draft it. Never store or repeat names, phone numbers or other personal information.
ChatGPT Mil: set your custom instructions onceSettings, then Personalization, then Custom instructions.
I am a staff officer. Write in plain English for busy leaders. Always start with a one or two sentence bottom line up front. Use short numbered points instead of long paragraphs. If you are not sure about something, tell me instead of guessing, and list what I should check.
ChatGPT Mil: shape an idea into a one-page conceptSet reasoning to High first. Good for early concepts and requirements, not code.
Help me turn a rough idea into a one-page concept for my boss. The idea: a shared, unclassified tracker so every section can see upcoming training, room bookings and vehicle requests in one place. Write it with these headings: Problem, Proposed solution, Who uses it, What success looks like in 90 days, Risks, and Decisions needed. Ask me up to three questions first if something important is missing.
Gemini: research with sourcesUse a public topic so the search can help.
Find the most recent public guidance on running effective after action reviews in a small unit. Summarize it in five bullets a new team leader can use tomorrow. Give the source and date for each bullet, and tell me which points you could not confirm.
Gemini: build your first agentPaste into Agent Designer's description box.
Create an agent called "Meeting Notes to Action Items." It turns rough, unclassified meeting notes into a one-page summary: the meeting purpose in one sentence, decisions made, a table of tasks (task, owner by role, due date, status) and open questions. It uses only the notes provided, marks missing details as TBD, and never includes names or personal information.
Gemini: turn messy notes into a trip reportUpload your notes. A photo of handwritten notes works too.
Turn the attached notes into a trip report I can download as a Word document. Use these headings: purpose, key engagements, observations, decisions, action items (a table with task, owner by role and due date) and follow-ups. Use only what is in my notes, mark anything missing as TBD, and leave out names and personal information.
Gemini: deep research on your own filesChoose Deep Research, attach several unclassified documents, then run this.
Read all the attached documents and write a report on what they agree on, where they conflict, and what is missing. Cite the document and section for every point, so I can check it. End with three questions I should ask before making a decision.
Gemini: a manager agent with a small teamAgent Designer. Then use the Preview tab to test it before you share it.
Create a manager agent called "Staff Product Builder." When I give it a topic and unclassified source files, it assigns the work to three sub-agents: a researcher that pulls the key facts from the files, an analyst that lists options with pros, cons and risks, and a writer that turns the result into a one-page information paper with a bottom line up front. The manager checks that every fact traces back to a source file and flags anything it can't confirm.
Grok: see why web search mattersAsk this twice: once with the Web switch off, then on. Compare the answers.
What is today's date, who is the current President of the United States, and what is the most recent date your information covers?
Grok: make a real slide deckClick + in the chat box, add the PowerPoint skill, then run this.
Make a five-slide PowerPoint for a 10-minute leader brief on a notional office move. Slide 1: title and bottom line. Slide 2: why we're moving. Slide 3: the timeline as a simple table. Slide 4: risks and how we reduce them. Slide 5: decisions needed from the commander. Plain style, large text, no more than four bullets a slide.
Grok: set up a daily automationOpen a project, choose Automations, and use this as the task.
Every weekday at 0700, search the web for public news from the last 24 hours about [a public topic your section tracks]. Write a half-page roundup: a two-sentence bottom line, then up to five bullets, each with its source and date. End with one line on anything that may need a leader's attention. Use public sources only.
Grok: build your own skillTurn on Skill creator, then describe the skill you want.
Help me build a skill called "Executive Summary." When I give it a document, it writes a summary of no more than 200 words for a senior leader: a bottom line up front, three key points, and the decision or action needed. It uses plain English, avoids jargon and filler phrases, and flags anything in the document that looks out of date. Ask me any questions you need before you build it.
Grok: teach it your format onceSkill creator. Attach one good, unclassified example of the product first.
Help me build a skill called "Our Memo Format." Use the attached example as the template: copy its headings, order, length and tone exactly. When I give it rough notes, it fills in that format, keeps every fact from my notes, marks anything missing as TBD, and adds nothing I didn't provide. Ask me any questions you need before you build it.
Grok: analyze a spreadsheetSwitch to Expert mode and upload an unclassified Excel or CSV file. It may take a few minutes.
Analyze the attached spreadsheet. Tell me in three sentences what it shows, then list the three biggest trends or outliers with the numbers behind them. Build one chart that shows the most important trend, and give me a cleaned-up Excel file I can download. Tell me any data problems you found, such as blanks or duplicates.
Grok: think a decision throughSwitch to Expert mode first.
Help me compare two options for a notional office move. Option A: move all at once over one weekend. Option B: move one section per week for four weeks. Build a short decision matrix on mission impact, cost, risk and workload. Then argue against your own recommendation in three sentences and tell me what information would change your answer.
All four: twenty ideas, ranked by how obvious they areWorks in any tool. Replace the bracket with your problem.
Give me 20 ideas for [your problem, for example: getting more junior leaders to use our office's AI tools]. For each idea, estimate the percent chance that a typical AI assistant would suggest it, from 0 to 100 percent. Sort the list from most likely to least likely. At least eight ideas should be under 10 percent. Then pick the two low-percentage ideas you think are most worth trying, and tell me in one sentence each why.
All four: same prompt, side by sideRun this in each tool and compare the answers.
In 120 words or less, explain to a new staff officer what CUI is, what it is not, and one common mistake people make when handling it. End with one question I should ask my security manager.

Try it in 3 steps

  1. On your government computer, open the tool: genai.mil with your CAC, or the Copilot tab in Teams.
  2. Copy one seed prompt above and paste it in.
  3. Check the answer. Fix one thing in the prompt and run it again.
